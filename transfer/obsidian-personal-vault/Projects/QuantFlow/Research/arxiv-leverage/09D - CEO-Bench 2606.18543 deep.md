# CEO-Bench (2606.18543) — deep read

**Actual title:** "CEO-Bench: Can Agents Play the Long Game?" **Authors:** Haozhe Chen, Karthik Narasimhan, Zhuang Liu. The `/pdf/2606.18543` URL named in the task yields only a thin, generic-sounding abstract when summarized directly; the full mechanism below comes from the HTML full text, which is internally detailed and consistent (specific equations, a 33-page PDF confirmed present on disk during retrieval).

See also: [[09 - CEO-Bench 2606.18543]] (first-pass note — still correct) · [[01D - ActiveGraph 2605.21997 deep]] and [[07D - Endless Terminals 2601.16443 deep]] (the same "independent evaluator" principle, arrived at three separate ways).

## 1. What the paper actually does

A from-scratch, mechanistically-simulated fictional SaaS company ("NovaMind") that an LLM agent operates for **500 simulated days**, starting with $1,000,000 cash and zero customers; cash below $0 is bankruptcy and ends the run. The agent acts through **34 tools across seven categories**: pricing/monetization (prices, tiers, quotas, promotions, in-product ads), growth/marketing (ad spend by channel × customer group, lead promotions), product/R&D (funding development, launching stochastic-timeline research projects), operations/reliability (infrastructure capacity, support funding), enterprise sales (multi-turn price/plan negotiation), information (paid market research to discover customer segments), and communication (monitoring/posting on simulated social media).

Underneath, an economic simulator the agent cannot read or influence except through those 34 tools: **26 customer groups**, each containing individual synthetic customers with private (agent-unobservable) preference parameters — willingness to pay, quality thresholds, usage demand, ad/support sensitivity. Subscription is a stated rule: accept a plan if price is under budget ceiling **and** perceived quality clears a bar that itself rises with price. Revenue, perceived quality, and customer acquisition are each governed by an explicit equation (cash changes by payments plus ad revenue minus a stated list of cost line items; perceived quality is a tier multiplier over base-plus-invested-quality minus outage/overload/issue/quota penalties plus relationship/tenure bonuses; acquisition scales with reputation × market saturation × seasonal/macro cycles × social sentiment × ad/referral pipeline). Competitors apply adaptive pressure on quality; customer segments drift in price sensitivity at different rates; reputation damage in one group spills into adjacent groups. The agent only ever observes what a real operator plausibly could — dashboards, database records, social posts, research reports, negotiation transcripts — never the underlying private parameters or equations. Scoring is simply cash at day 500, or days survived if bankrupt. Different simulator subsystems draw from **independent random number generators**, so a fixed seed reproduces the same downstream world state exactly (e.g., repeated market-research calls surface new segments in the same order every time).

The sentence that matters most for QuantFlow: **"Almost all simulator outcomes are generated by explicit mechanisms rather than by using an LLM as an opaque judge."** The grader is not an LLM asked "did the agent do a good job" — it is an independently coded economic model the agent cannot see, and the "correct" outcome falls out of that model's own arithmetic, not out of anyone's judgment call.

## 2. Evidence quality

One benchmark, one fictional environment, ten models plus a hand-written rule-based baseline plus an analytically estimated upper bound (~$2.2B — a soft, author-chosen construction: sum maximum supportable revenue per segment minus required costs, then discount for "execution friction," itself a fudge factor rather than a derivation). Results: only two of ten tested models (Claude Opus 4.8 at $27.8M, GPT-5.5 at $21.3M) finished above their own starting cash on their best-of-three run; several models (GLM 5.1, Grok 4.20, DeepSeek V4 Pro) went bankrupt in most or all runs, ending with single-digit-to-low-hundreds of dollars. Striking and double-edged: the **rule-based baseline ($15.8M) beat eight of the ten LLM agents** — real evidence about current agents' long-horizon planning, but also unresolved ambiguity the authors don't rule out: does the simulated economy actually reward genuine strategy, or a narrow, gameable heuristic more than genuine strategy? Only **three runs per model** — thin for a stochastic 500-day simulation, and variance is high: GPT-5.5's survival days were 333.7 ± 229.7, a standard deviation nearly as large as the mean. The authors' own stated limitations are direct and worth quoting: product quality is collapsed to a single scalar because they "have not found a reliable way to evaluate a model's capability to propose qualitative changes to products"; compliance, security, and fundraising are left out entirely for tractability; and they explicitly name a "reality gap" between simulation and an actual business. Net: one clean, well-justified design principle (mechanistic-not-LLM grading) riding on top of thin, high-variance, single-domain evidence about an environment openly acknowledged as a simplification. Compelling idea, thin evidence — label it as such.

## 3. The transferable pattern

(a) **The evaluator must be built independently of, and unreadable by, the thing it evaluates** — a coded mechanism with its own private state and equations, never an LLM asked to opine, never a mirror of the agent's own assumptions. This is a third independent confirmation (after ActiveGraph's strict replay and Endless Terminals' frontier-model solvability check) of the same underlying principle: real verification needs a second source that cannot share the first source's blind spots.

(b) **Splitting evaluation into one hard pass/fail condition and separate soft, multi-period diagnostic metrics** (survival days, bankruptcy count, mean vs. max across repeated runs, recovery after reputation damage) rather than collapsing everything into one number — useful specifically because delayed consequences (churn "surfacing later" from an earlier quality mistake) are invisible in a single end-state score.

## 4. Applies to QuantFlow how

Not to the ingest rungs (WO-107/108) — nothing here concerns scraping a hostile external surface. The fit is WO-109 and WO-110. For WO-109's founder-stated risk (does agent collaboration actually work, not just narrate working), CEO-Bench's model is a template for the **synthetic Mission used as cold QA** already on [[09 - CEO-Bench 2606.18543]]'s steal checklist: run the orchestrator/worker loop against a scripted calendar of synthetic MarketEvents and Datasets whose "correct" downstream numbers are computed by a small, independent, hand-audited script the agents cannot read — mirroring "explicit mechanisms, not an opaque judge" exactly. This read's addition is the concrete shape: the checker must be a separate calculation over the synthetic data, never a re-derivation from the same generator that produced the Mission, or it inherits the schema-vs-schema blindness the Kernel just demonstrated.

For WO-110's critic design, the hard/soft split maps directly onto what SCOPES.md already separates: the **hard gate** stays exactly what WO-110 specifies (`publish_artifact` refuses `kind: "report"` without a linked evaluation whose `verdict === "supports"`) — if anything, CEO-Bench's evidence argues *for* keeping that binary and mechanical, since its own one-number (cash) outcome is what a hand-written heuristic already beat most frontier agents on. The **soft metrics** (recovery from a bad call, consistency of a conclusion across repeated runs, whether a strategy that looked good on one Dataset slice held up on another) belong as additional properties on `evaluation.metrics`, informational only, never a second path to a passing verdict — again already on the existing note's checklist ("hard gates vs soft horizon metrics"). This read's contribution is the causal *why*: CEO-Bench shows mistakes are often invisible until several steps after they're made (reputation propagating to churn later), the same shape as the schema/DB mismatch that caused the WO-V1 wound — the failure was live for an unmeasured period before anything detected it. A soft metric that specifically scores "did an earlier claim get revisited and corrected once new data arrived," not just "is the final report clean," is the CEO-Bench-shaped defense against a QuantFlow-shaped version of the same delayed-failure pattern.

## 5. Where it conflicts with doctrine

No chassis risk — NovaMind is a bespoke Python simulation, not a runtime anyone would install beside the Kernel. The one place to watch: if a future WO-109/110 synthetic-Mission checker were ever implemented as an LLM asked to grade the run, that violates "never your own verifier" in exactly the way the paper itself warns against ("rather than using an LLM as an opaque judge"). The checker must be a hand-written, independently reasoned calculation over the synthetic dataset, mirroring CEO-Bench's own choice, or it inherits the same blindness the schema/DB gates just demonstrated.

`read-again-when` WO-110's Evaluation schema extension is actually being drafted — the hard/soft metric split and the "mechanism, not opaque judge" line are worth having open at that exact moment, but there is nothing further to extract from this paper before then; its evidence is too thin and its domain too distant (fictional SaaS economics) to justify a second full read for any other purpose.
